## The thesis

The workplace's decline as a center of real collaboration didn’t begin with COVID, remote work, or even open offices and Slack. It began earlier, and more quietly, when one of the last unsanctioned collaboration rituals disappeared: the cigarette break.

Stripped of nostalgia or health debates, smoking functioned as a collaboration technology. It created a recurring, illegitimate pause in the workday where people from across roles and hierarchies gathered without an agenda. Executives, assistants, engineers, union workers—people who rarely shared formal meetings—shared a small, bounded moment. Seniority flattened. Conversation wandered. Information flowed sideways instead of up or down.

Most importantly, it was off the record. No notes. No outcomes. No visibility. That made it feel safe.

The decline of smoking removed the ritual but not the function. Scheduled meetings, wellness initiatives, and digital collaboration tools replaced it, but none of these recreated the essential ingredients: weak ties, status ambiguity, and informal sensemaking. Collaboration became productive, legible, and measurable and, in the process, less human.

By the time COVID forced work out of the office, the informal connective tissue was already thin. Remote work didn’t kill collaboration; it exposed how long we’d been relying on formal structures to do a job once handled by casual proximity and shared interruption.

The challenge now is designing a new collaboration ritual that restores what we lost without forcing everyone back into a half-empty office. A modern equivalent wouldn’t mimic smoking itself, but its properties.

A viable remote ritual would be opt-in, recurring, and officially unofficial. It would interrupt the workday rather than sit alongside it. Participation would cut across teams and levels by default, not by invitation. Crucially, it would produce nothing—no artifacts, no action items, no follow-ups.

It wouldn’t scale neatly, and that’s the point. It would be lightly inconvenient, slightly inefficient, and socially ambiguous. Over time, it would rebuild weak ties, shared context, and informal trust: the raw materials collaboration depends on, but formal systems can’t create by fiat.

The cigarette break worked not because it was healthy or productive, but because it was human, interruptive, and shared. If remote work is to support real collaboration, it needs its own version of that pause. It needs a place where nothing is expected, and therefore something unexpected can happen.

## What I tried

I started with the thesis. I have been very nostalgic for smoking for years - and this seems to be a trend now. Looking back on my work experiences, I remember smoke breaks as where I made and expanded some of my best early work relationships. Then I started thinking about the actual function they played in the workplace, turning that into the Smoke Break Thesis.

I spent considerable time trying to spec out what a "digital smoke break” could look like. Ultimately, I concluded you can’t recreate it digitally, or at least I couldn’t.

As a big fan of conceptual art, and especially instruction art, I thought… maybe this is just an art project in and of itself?
